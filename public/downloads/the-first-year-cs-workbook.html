    01 your programme

    Decode your programme

    Computer Science goes by different names depending on where you study: BSc Computer Science, BSc IT, BCom Informatics, Diploma in Computer Science, Diploma in IT. The building blocks underneath are mostly the same.

    • Every qualification has an NQF level. The National Qualifications Framework is how South Africa ranks qualifications. It decides what you can study next.
    • Every module has a credit value, and one credit is 10 hours of work. Those are "notional hours": lectures, practicals, tutorials, assignments, self-study and exams all count. A 16-credit module expects about 160 hours from you.
    • A full-time year is usually about 120 credits. That's roughly 1,200 hours, or 35 to 40 hours a week across the teaching and exam weeks. Your programme was designed like a full-time job.
    • Extended programmes add a year of support. You get foundation modules and more time, and you finish with the same qualification.
    • Some modules are locked behind others. These are prerequisites. Fail one and the next module waits, which can push your graduation back a semester or a year.

    02 the rules

    The rules nobody explains

    Every university has written rules that decide whether you can write the exam, what happens when you fail, and when you can be excluded. Most students read them after something has gone wrong. Read them now, while they still feel irrelevant.

    Exam entryDP, exam admission, minimum semester mark
    A minimum mark (sometimes attendance or practicals too) that you need during the semester before you're allowed into the exam. Miss it and the exam is gone, no matter how well you would have written.
    Semester markyear mark, predicate, CA mark
    The marks from your tests, practicals and assignments. It usually counts toward your final mark together with the exam.
    Final mark
    Usually a weighted mix, like 50% semester mark and 50% exam, or 40/60. Each module's study guide gives the split.
    Sub-minimum
    A minimum you need in the exam itself. Some modules fail you for missing it even if your final mark is above the pass mark.
    Supplementary exam"sup"
    A second shot at the exam, usually offered when your final mark lands just below the pass mark. The range and the rules differ per university, so check yours.
    Continuous assessment
    Some modules have no final exam. Every test and practical counts, so there's no catching up at the end.
    Academic exclusion
    If you don't pass enough credits over time, the university can stop you from re-registering. The exact rule is in your yearbook.
    Funding rules
    NSFAS and most bursaries have their own academic progress rules on top of the university's. Failing modules can affect your funding, so know your funder's rule as well as the university's.
    Plagiarism and AI
    CS departments run code through similarity checkers, and many now have written AI policies. Submitting a friend's practical is the fastest way to a disciplinary hearing.

    03 the subjects

    What you'll actually study

    Module names change between universities, but first-year CS comes down to a handful of subject families. Each family needs a different way of studying, and using the wrong method is how hard-working students still fail.

    Programming

    Java, Python, C++ or C#, depending on where you study. It's a skill, like a language or a sport, and practicals build on each other week after week.

    Study it by typing every example yourself and redoing practicals from a blank file.

    Mathematics

    Calculus, algebra, statistics and discrete maths (logic, sets, proofs). In many BSc programmes this is the module that quietly decides who continues.

    Study it by doing problems every day. Reading worked solutions feels like learning and mostly isn't.

    Computer systems

    How the machine works under your code: binary and hex, memory, CPUs, operating systems, networks.

    Study it by drawing diagrams from memory, using flashcards for terms, and practising conversions until they're automatic.

    Information systems and databases

    How organisations store and use data. Many programmes introduce SQL in first year.

    Study it by writing queries on real tables and designing small databases for things you know, like a spaza shop's stock or a taxi rank's routes.

    Web and applications

    HTML, CSS, sometimes JavaScript or app basics. Usually the most fun module, which is why people lose marks on the boring requirements.

    Study it by building small pages, and reading the marking rubric before you start.

    Communication and literacy

    Writing, referencing, presenting, sometimes computer literacy. These are the easiest credits in your year, and students drop them by not taking them seriously.

    Study it by following the rubric exactly and submitting early.

    04 programming

    Learning to code properly

    Programming is where first-years split into two groups. One group writes code every week. The other watches lectures, copies the practical from a friend or an AI, and meets the code properly for the first time in the test. The second group feels fine right up until the first big test.

    Three habits fix most of it:

    1. The blank-file test. Close your notes, open an empty file and write this week's topic from scratch: a loop that sums an array, a class with a constructor, whatever it is. If you can't, you've just found what to practise.
    2. The 20-minute rule. Stuck for 20 minutes? Write down what you expected, what actually happened and what you've tried. Then ask a tutor, a classmate or an AI. Writing it down solves it surprisingly often.
    3. AI as a tutor, never the author. Ask it to explain an error or a concept. Don't paste the practical in and submit what comes out. You'll pass the practical and fail the test.

    Know your exam format early. At some universities the programming exam is handwritten. At others it's on a lab computer with no internet. If yours is on paper, practise writing code on paper, because there's no autocomplete and nothing underlines your mistakes.

      06 assessments

      Tests and exams

      CS assessments come in a few formats, and each one needs different preparation:

      • Handwritten code. Practise on paper, timed. Learn the syntax cold.
      • On a computer, offline. Usually an IDE with no internet. Practise without relying on autocomplete, and know how to run and debug quickly.
      • Theory and multiple choice. Definitions, comparisons, conversions. Flashcards and past papers.
      • Projects and portfolios. Marks follow the rubric. Read it before you start and tick it off before you submit.

      Past papers are the closest thing to a cheat code that's allowed. Ask your class rep, tutors, seniors and the library. Write them under time, mark yourself honestly, and the topics you keep losing marks on become your study list.

      08 setbacks

      When it goes wrong

      Failing a test in first year is common, and it doesn't decide your degree. A failed module is a setback with a process for fixing it. The real damage comes from hiding it, from yourself, your funder or your family, until the options run out.

      1. Get the facts. Your actual marks, whether you qualify for a sup, and what the rules say about repeating.
      2. Find the cause honestly. Use the post-mortem below.
      3. Talk to someone this week. Your lecturer for the academic side, the faculty office for the rules, and the funding office if your funding could be affected.
      4. Make a plan with dates on it.

      If it's bigger than marks, like stress, money, home or your mental health, your campus counselling or wellness centre is there for registered students and is usually free. Go before it becomes an emergency.

      09 building

      Beyond the marks

      You don't need a startup or a GitHub full of green squares in first year. Marks come first. But one small project you build because you want to teaches you what practicals can't: planning, getting stuck with no memo to check, and finishing something.

      • Make a GitHub account and keep your own projects there. Keep assessed work private.
      • Build one thing you'd actually use. A budget tracker, a timetable app, a quiz for one of your modules.
      • Use the holidays. December and the mid-year break are when you can get ahead without a timetable fighting you.
